# Collaborating across disciplines

Good design ships through other people. Most collaboration problems come from unclear goals, unclear roles and late involvement, not from bad intentions.

## 1. Start every project the same way

Run a short kickoff with product, engineering and design (and research, content or data when involved):

- The problem, the users and the success metric (`framing-design-problems`), agreed by all.
- Constraints: time, technology, legal, brand.
- Roles: who decides what. A simple split works for most teams: product owns *what and why* and the priority, design owns *how it works and feels for users*, engineering owns *how it is built* and its feasibility, and the trio decides scope together. Write exceptions down.
- How you will work: check-in rhythm, where work is shared, how decisions are logged (`uxg decision`).

## 2. With product managers

- Share the problem space early, before solutions. Bring evidence (`researching-users-and-markets`, `analyzing-ux-data`), not only opinions.
- Translate design choices into the metrics the PM owns (`measuring-design-impact`).
- Negotiate scope with options, not ultimatums: "the full version, a smaller version that keeps the key benefit, and what we would lose".
- Agree on what "done" means for the user before the sprint, not after.

## 3. With engineers

- Involve an engineer during exploration; feasibility surprises at handoff are expensive.
- Design with the real components and tokens (`designing-with-design-systems`); ask what is cheap and what is costly.
- Hand off with every state, responsive rules and acceptance criteria (`preparing-handoff`), then stay available: pair on tricky parts instead of throwing files over a wall.
- Review the build before release with a design QA pass against the acceptance criteria; file issues with screenshots and severity. For a running app, `reviewing-ux-in-code` and `uxg scan` help both sides.
- Respect engineering constraints, and explain user impact when a shortcut would hurt people.

## 4. With other partners

| Partner | What helps |
|---|---|
| Research | Shared research questions, joint synthesis, design attends sessions. |
| Data and analytics | Agree on events and metric definitions before launch. |
| Content and UX writing | Real copy early; a shared glossary. |
| Marketing and brand | Shared brand rules; separate product clarity from campaign voice. |
| Legal and compliance | Bring them in at framing for regulated flows; design the best version within their constraints. |
| Support and sales | Mine tickets and calls for evidence; close the loop on fixes. |
| Leadership | Short, outcome-focused updates (`presenting-design-work`). |

## 5. When people disagree

Separate the question type (`receiving-design-feedback`): evidence, standards and constraints decide; taste is discussed against the goal. Propose the smallest test when nobody has evidence. Escalate as a shared question with options, never as a complaint about a person. Log the decision and move on.

## 6. By career level

- **Intern and junior:** ask questions early, show work in progress often, write down decisions and their reasons, learn the codebase and design system.
- **Mid-level:** own a feature end to end, run kickoffs and reviews, bring evidence to discussions.
- **Senior and lead:** shape the roadmap with product, set quality bars, unblock others, mentor.
- **Manager and head of design:** design the operating model and rituals between disciplines (`running-design-operations`), and represent design in leadership (`leading-design-teams`).

## 7. Remote and asynchronous

Write things down: decisions, rationale and open questions in shared docs; short recorded walkthroughs instead of long meetings; clear owners and deadlines for feedback.
