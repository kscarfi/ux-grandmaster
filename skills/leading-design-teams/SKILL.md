# Leading design teams

A design team is itself a design problem: the users are the designers and their partners, and the outcome is better products. Start from the business goals and the current state, not from another company's org chart.

## 1. Understand the context

Ask, one question at a time: company stage and size, products and platforms, current designers and where they sit, how design is involved today (early in strategy, or late for visuals), the top business goals for the next year, and the biggest complaints about design from inside and outside the team.

Place the organization on a design maturity scale. InVision's *The New Design Frontier* report describes five levels, from design as visual production to design as part of business strategy; use it to describe where the team is and the next step, not to grade people.

## 2. Structure

| Model | How it works | Works well when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Centralized** | One design team takes requests from across the company | Small teams, early stage, consistency matters most | Designers far from product teams; becomes a service desk |
| **Embedded** | Designers sit in product teams and report there | Fast product teams with strong product leaders | Inconsistency, isolation, no design career path |
| **Hybrid** | Designers work inside product teams but report to design leadership | Most growing organizations | Needs clear rules for who sets priorities day to day |

The hybrid model is described in many forms, for example in InVision's *Design Engineering Handbook* and in Peter Merholz and Kristin Skinner's *Org Design for Design Orgs*. Choose based on context and revisit as the company grows.

## 3. Hiring

- Write the hiring plan from the roadmap: which product areas need which skills (product design, research, content, design systems, design engineering, DesignOps), and in what order.
- Define each role by outcomes and level expectations (`growing-design-careers`), not a tool list.
- Interview loop: portfolio review focused on process and decisions, a collaborative exercise close to real work, a partner interview with product and engineering, and a values conversation. Use the same rubric for every candidate.
- Avoid unpaid take-home work that takes more than a few hours.

## 4. Running the team

- **Rituals:** regular critique, design reviews at set milestones, team sharing, one-to-ones. See `running-design-operations`.
- **Quality bar:** a shared definition of done for design (the gates in `using-ux-grandmaster`), a design system (`building-design-systems`), and design QA before release.
- **Growth:** career ladder, feedback, mentoring, promotion criteria (`growing-design-careers`).
- **Partnership:** a design counterpart for every product and engineering lead (`collaborating-across-disciplines`).
- **AI:** a clear policy and shared tooling (`operating-ai-in-design`).

## 5. Showing value

Tie design work to the company's metrics (`measuring-design-impact`): conversion, retention, support contacts, time to complete key tasks, accessibility compliance, delivery speed. Report outcomes, not output. For a headcount or budget case, show the problem, the cost of not solving it, the proposal and the expected impact, with evidence (`presenting-design-work`).

## 6. A new leader's first months

1. **Listen:** one-to-ones with every designer and key partners; review recent work, research and metrics.
2. **Diagnose:** write down strengths, problems and opportunities; share it back.
3. **Agree on priorities** with leadership: two or three goals for the next two quarters.
4. **Deliver a few visible improvements** while starting longer changes (structure, ladder, process).
