# Running design operations

DesignOps exists so designers spend their time on design and the organization gets more value from it. Start from the team's real pain, not from a framework.

## 1. Diagnose first

Ask, one question at a time, or read what exists:

- Team size, reporting lines and how designers are placed (central, embedded in product teams, or a mix).
- Where time goes: count a typical week. Meetings, waiting for decisions, rework, searching for files, tooling friction.
- The three biggest complaints from designers and from partners (product, engineering).
- What is already in place: rituals, templates, a design system, a research practice, tool admin.

Write the diagnosis as problems with evidence ("handoff questions take 6 meetings per feature, from the last two releases"), not as missing best practices.

## 2. The three areas

A common way to organize DesignOps is around how people work together, how work gets done, and how work creates impact (InVision's *DesignOps Handbook*; Nielsen Norman Group's DesignOps writing). In practice:

| Area | What to look at |
|---|---|
| **People** | Hiring plan and interview loop, onboarding, career ladder and levels, capacity and allocation, rituals for growth (mentoring, critique), well-being and workload. See `leading-design-teams` and `growing-design-careers`. |
| **Process** | Intake and prioritization of design requests, the design process per project size (see `using-ux-grandmaster` scales), critique and review cadence, decision logs, design QA before release, handoff, documentation. |
| **Tools and assets** | Tool stack and licenses, file structure and naming, library and design system governance (`building-design-systems`), research repository (`running-research-operations`), AI tools (`operating-ai-in-design`). |
| **Measurement** | Design's impact on product metrics (`measuring-design-impact`), team health (survey), throughput and cycle time where it helps, design debt. |

## 3. Pick a few interventions

Recommend **at most three** changes, the ones that most directly fix the problems the person named. A long list of good practices is the most common failure here: it reads as thorough and gets nothing done. Prefer small pilots with one team over org-wide rollouts.

Use this format for the answer:

> **What I think is going on:** one or two sentences, with the assumptions you made and how to check them.
>
> **Change 1: [name].** Fixes: [which stated problem]. Owner: [role]. First step this week: [action]. You will know it works when: [signal].
>
> **Change 2 …** (same fields)
>
> **Later, if these work:** one line listing other ideas, no detail.

Examples of changes that fit:

- A weekly critique with a fixed format (`critiquing-designs`) instead of ad hoc feedback.
- A one-page intake form and a visible design backlog shared with product.
- A design QA step before release with acceptance criteria from `preparing-handoff`.
- File and library conventions plus a quarterly cleanup.

## 4. Templates

Offer ready-to-use artifacts when they fit: intake form, critique agenda, design review checklist, decision log (`uxg decision`), project kickoff agenda, handoff checklist, tool inventory with owners and costs, team health survey. Write them in the team's language and keep each to one page.

## 5. Measure and revisit

Agree on how success is measured before the change, check after a fixed period, and keep or drop the change based on that. Report results to leadership in business terms (`presenting-design-work`).
