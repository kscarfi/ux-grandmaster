# Presenting design work

A presentation is a design for an audience. Decide what you need from them, then show only what gets you there.

## 1. Before building anything

Answer three questions:

1. **Who is in the room, and what do they care about?** Executives: outcomes, risk, cost, time. Product: goals and trade-offs. Engineering: feasibility and scope. Designers: craft and rationale.
2. **What do you need from them?** A decision, feedback on something specific, awareness, or approval. Say it at the start.
3. **What stage is the work?** Early ideas invite direction; near-final work invites detail. Tell the audience which kind of feedback helps now.

## 2. Structure

1. **The ask** in one sentence.
2. **The problem and why it matters**, with evidence and the success metric.
3. **What we considered** (the directions from `diverging-concepts`), briefly, and why this one.
4. **The design**, walked through as a user story on real screens, not a component tour.
5. **Risks, trade-offs and open questions**, stated plainly.
6. **The ask again**, with the decision needed and by when.

For executives, compress to one slide or a short memo: problem, recommendation, impact on the metric, cost and risk, decision needed.

## 3. Show it well

- Real screens with real copy and data, refined with `refining-generated-designs`.
- One idea per slide or section. Use the user's journey as the order.
- Show numbers with their source and uncertainty; never present a guess as a measurement.
- Prepare answers for the obvious objections, with evidence.

## 4. Running a review or critique

Set the scope ("feedback on the flow, not the visual details"), name a facilitator and a note taker, ask each person for their top concern, and end with decisions and owners. Afterwards, log decisions with `uxg decision` and handle feedback with `receiving-design-feedback`.

## 5. Written updates

Weekly or project updates: what changed, what was decided, what is blocked and what you need, in five lines or fewer, with links for detail.
